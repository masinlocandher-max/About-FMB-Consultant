For a long time it was tempting to blame the advice. It is easier to say, ‘I did it because they told me to.’ And yes, people influenced me. Some advice was bad for my life even if it was given with good intentions.

But I was the one who followed it.

That sentence took me longer to accept than it should have.

I know myself well enough to admit that I have done wrong things. I have made drastic decisions. I have made choices I would never recommend to another person. I have hurt myself with my own judgment and complicated my own journey. I do not need to make those mistakes prettier now just because I survived them.

And believe it or not, she stayed.

My mother stayed.

Merlita E. Bautista.

She stayed when I was right and when I was absolutely not right. She stayed through decisions she disagreed with. She stayed through versions of me that were difficult, stubborn, emotional, confused, and sometimes very sure of myself while heading in the wrong direction.

She fought with me. She also fought for me.

Tell me that is not love.

I do not mean the kind of love that applauds everything you do. That is easy when things are going well. I mean the person who can tell you that you are wrong and still refuse to leave you alone inside the damage. The person who can be angry, disappointed, worried, tired, and still be there.

For years I used the words best friend for other people before I understood that my real best friend had been much closer to home.

My answer happens to be my mother. I know that answer will hurt for some readers rather than comfort them.

Not everybody has a safe mother. Some people lost theirs. Some never knew theirs. Some had to build distance from a parent in order to become healthy. Some found family in people who share none of their blood.

I am not telling you that a mother automatically deserves loyalty. Blood is not proof of safety.

I am telling you to notice the person who stayed.

Maybe it is your father. A sibling. A partner. A cousin. A friend who became family. Maybe it is somebody you met much later. Maybe you are still waiting to meet them.

Look for the person who tells you the truth without trying to own you. The person who can disagree without making abandonment the punishment. The person who does not need you to become smaller, easier, richer, prettier, more successful, or more convenient before they can care about what happens to you.

Then do something I wish I had learned earlier: love them, listen to them, but do not make them responsible for choosing your life.

Advice is still advice, even when it comes from someone you love.

The consequence will still know your address.

My mother could stay beside me. She could fight for me. She could warn me. She could love me through things I had done wrong.

She still could not live the consequence for me.

That part was mine.

Maybe that is another form of love too. Someone can stay without taking away your responsibility to grow.

This chapter is for my mother.

But it is really about the person who stayed.

Find yours. And when you do, do not wait for a disaster to realize what you have.

# PART III: WANT MORE WITHOUT APOLOGY

---

## 08. HINDI AKO SAGABAL SA AMBISYON NINUMAN

There are people who will experience your ability as a threat even when you have done nothing to threaten them.

Sometimes you are simply fast. You see an opening and move. You notice a problem and begin solving it. You know how to connect people, ideas, timing, and resources. You are madiskarte.

To you, that may feel like initiative. To someone who is already afraid of being outpaced, it can look like competition.

This is where I return to a line I have always found powerful: Hindi ako sagabal sa ambisyon ninuman.

I am not an obstacle to anyone’s ambition.

The line matters to me because it names a way of moving through the world that ambition often forgets. I do not need another person’s opportunity to disappear for mine to exist. I do not need to hide information so I can remain the most useful person in the room. I do not need to make somebody smaller so my own ability can be seen.

If you are better than me at something, be better.

If you see an opportunity I missed, take it.

If you can build something stronger, build it.

I can respect your ambition without surrendering mine.

That last sentence is where the difficulty begins.

People sometimes hear “make room for others” and translate it into “remove yourself.” They confuse humility with self-erasure. They ask capable people to become less visible so everybody else can feel comfortable.

I do not believe in that kind of generosity.

You do not have to perform incompetence to appear kind. You do not have to refuse an opportunity you earned because somebody else wanted it too. You do not have to apologize for being prepared, resourceful, articulate, fast, creative, or good at what you do.

You do, however, have to remain honest about how you use those strengths.

There is a difference between being madiskarte and exploiting people who have less information. There is a difference between confidence and entitlement. There is a difference between taking initiative and taking credit. There is a difference between entering a room with certainty and acting as if nobody else belongs there.

Competence does not excuse arrogance.

I have seen ambition become ugly when scarcity gets into it. People begin guarding contacts as if another person’s introduction makes their own relationships less valuable. They withhold information that costs them nothing to share. They make access unnecessarily difficult. They treat collaboration as a risk to their identity because being “the only one” has become part of how they feel important.

That kind of ambition is fragile.

Secure ambition can survive witnesses.

It can say, “I want this,” without requiring somebody else’s humiliation. It can compete hard and still recognize good work. It can lose without rewriting the winner into a villain. It can win without needing to remind the person who lost.

This is more difficult than it sounds because envy is ordinary. We all feel it.

There are moments when another person’s success lands directly on an insecurity. You wanted the same opportunity. You worked hard too. You may even believe you were more qualified. You do not have to pretend to be delighted.

But you do have to decide what disappointment is allowed to make of you.

Envy can become useful if you are willing to read it.

What exactly hurts?

The money? The recognition? The access? The freedom? The quality of the work? The courage it took? The fact that they began later and arrived sooner?

Sometimes envy tells the truth about desire more clearly than our goals do.

Use the information.

Do not turn it into sabotage.

Hindi ako sagabal sa ambisyon ninuman.

That is not a promise to disappear. It is a promise not to build my ambition out of somebody else’s obstruction.

There is room for me to want more without needing you to have less.

I know what it is like to move quickly because my mind often moves from problem to possible solution before the conversation has finished describing the problem.

That can be useful.

It can also be irritating.

Madiskarte is not automatically a compliment if the person using it means you are always three steps ahead and everybody else is tired of catching up.

I had to learn that being fast does not give me ownership of the road.

Sometimes another person needs time because they are careful, not incapable. Sometimes I see a route quickly because I have information they were never given. Sometimes I can connect resources because I have spent years building a network that makes the connection look easier than it actually is.

Remembering that keeps confidence from becoming contempt.

I do not want to make myself slower to protect another person’s insecurity.

I do want to become better at bringing people with me when the work requires a team.

That is a different kind of strength.

The person who can do everything alone may look impressive for a while. The person who can make a group stronger without needing to be the only source of value builds something that can outlive her mood, schedule, and availability.

Ambition matured for me when I stopped asking only, “How far can I go?” and started asking, “What kind of environment am I creating while I go there?”

## 09. WHEN COMPETENCE MAKES PEOPLE UNCOMFORTABLE

Being good at something changes the social temperature of a room.

People respond to competence in complicated ways. Some are relieved by it. Some are curious. Some want to learn. Some become competitive. Some become defensive before you have even challenged them.

The response is not always about you.

That is useful to remember because capable people can waste a great deal of energy trying to make everybody comfortable with their capability.

You soften every statement. Add disclaimers to things you know. Pretend not to care about an opportunity you clearly want. Give away the idea before anybody can accuse you of wanting credit. Make jokes about your own ability so nobody has to feel intimidated.

At first, it can look like social intelligence.

Eventually it feels like disappearance.

There is wisdom in reading the room. Expertise without awareness can become unbearable. Not every conversation needs your correction. Not every silence is evidence that people need your opinion. A person can know a great deal and still have poor judgment about when that knowledge is useful.

But reading the room should not become shrinking for the room.

There are spaces where people are unsettled not because you behaved badly but because you arrived prepared. Your clarity exposes their vagueness. Your speed exposes delay. Your question reveals that an assumption was never tested. Your willingness to do the work makes an old excuse harder to maintain.

It is tempting to make yourself less capable so the tension disappears.

Do not.

Become more precise instead.

Know what you know. Know what you do not know. Speak from evidence when evidence is available. Give credit. Ask questions before assuming. Let other people finish. Do not use intelligence as theatre.

Then stop apologizing for the rest.

There is a quiet arrogance in assuming that another adult cannot survive your competence unless you disguise it. Let people have their own reactions.

Some of the discomfort around strong people comes from history. A person may have worked in rooms where confidence was used as domination. They may be cautious for good reason. Some spaces are not neutral either. Appearance, accent, gender, age, social class, education, reputation, and who introduced you can all shape how ability is interpreted before the work is even examined.

The answer is not to pretend those dynamics do not exist.

Preparation becomes strategy.

Know your material. Know your numbers. Know what you can prove and what you only feel. Know the weaknesses in your own case before somebody else finds them. If you are entering a room where people may underestimate you, substance is a better defense than performance.

You cannot control whether somebody decides your confidence is arrogance. You can control whether your confidence has earned its foundation.

That is the standard I care about more now.

Not appearing impressive.

Being prepared enough that I do not need to pretend.

## 10. THE ROOM IS BIG ENOUGH

Scarcity changes people’s manners.

When opportunity feels limited, generosity begins to look expensive. People guard information, introductions, credit, access, visibility, even simple encouragement. They fear that opening a door for somebody else will make the room more crowded for them.

Sometimes the fear is rational. Not every opportunity is abundant. There is only one role. One contract. One seat. One budget. One winner.

Pretending everything is infinitely available can become dishonest.

But scarcity in one moment does not have to become a personality.

You can compete for a specific opportunity without turning life into permanent competition.

This matters because some of the most valuable things we can give another person are not things that diminish when shared.

Information can be shared. Credit can be accurate. An introduction can be made. A person’s name can be mentioned in a room where they are not present. A useful process can be explained. Someone can be encouraged without your own ability decreasing by one percentage point.

The room may not literally have infinite seats, but a healthy ecosystem creates new rooms.

That is what I find more interesting than being the only one inside an old one.

There are people who build influence by controlling access. There are others who build influence by becoming the person who helps good people find one another. Both can look powerful from the outside. Only one creates something larger than itself.

Making room does not mean becoming endlessly available. Generosity without judgment becomes another form of self-neglect. You are allowed to protect confidential information. You are allowed to keep boundaries around relationships you worked hard to build. You do not owe every stranger your contacts, strategy, time, or endorsement.

There is a difference between gatekeeping and stewardship.
