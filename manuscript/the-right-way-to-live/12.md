Sometimes we help because we care.

Sometimes we help because being needed makes us feel secure.

Usually there is a mixture.

Knowing yourself means being willing to look at the mixture without turning every imperfect motive into a reason for shame.

You can care and enjoy being appreciated. You can want to contribute and also want recognition. You can be generous and still need boundaries. Human motives are rarely pure.

The goal is not purity.

The goal is honesty strong enough to keep the motive from controlling you.

When I say para sa sarili, I mean building a life I do not secretly resent.

I mean not volunteering for every burden and then becoming angry that nobody stopped me.

I mean not performing strength until I no longer know how to ask for help.

I mean protecting my own ambitions instead of always making them the first thing sacrificed when somebody else has a need.

This is where the three directions correct one another.

Para sa bayan without para sa sarili can become martyrdom.

Para sa sarili without para sa kapwa can become indifference.

Para sa kapwa without boundaries can become resentment.

There is no perfect balance that holds in every season. Of course there is not.

Some seasons will ask more from one direction than another. There may be years when family comes first, months when work demands more, days when the country feels urgent, seasons when your own survival has to become the priority.

Balance is not an equal division of hours.

It is the ability to notice when one part of your life has begun consuming all the others.

Para sa sarili is not permission to stop caring.

It is how I make sure caring does not eventually turn me into somebody who has nothing left but bitterness.

Caring for myself also includes enjoying the life I worked to build.

This sounds obvious, yet ambitious people can become strangely suspicious of pleasure. Every peso should become an investment. Every free hour should become productivity. Rest has to be justified by exhaustion. Beauty has to be defended as confidence. Travel has to become content or education or networking before it is allowed to be fun.

I do not want that.

Some things can simply be enjoyed.

A good meal.

A beautiful room.

Clothes I like.

A perfume that makes an ordinary day feel different.

A trip.

A quiet morning.

A cosmetic choice I wanted and could afford.

Time with people I love without turning the moment into material.

Hard work should build more than the ability to work harder.

It should eventually buy some freedom, some comfort, some choice, some room to experience the life you were working for.

Otherwise success becomes another employer you can never resign from.

## 33. I ONCE THOUGHT I WOULD ENTER POLITICS

I want to say this plainly because I have thought about it in different forms for years: there was a time when I could imagine myself in politics.

Not because I was fascinated by titles.

The title was never the interesting part.

What attracted me was the possibility of scale. If you care about people, public office looks like one of the places where care can become structure. A private person can help one family. A good policy can affect thousands. A project can solve a local problem. A decision made by government can change the conditions that created the problem in the first place.

That kind of power is difficult to ignore if you are drawn to building things and fixing systems.

I could imagine it.

Then, over time, I became less romantic about the route.

I began to understand that wanting to serve and wanting to survive political life are not the same ambition.

Politics asks for things beyond competence. It asks for alliances. Timing. Compromise. Loyalty. Money. Endurance. The ability to work with people you may not trust because they still represent constituencies, institutions, or votes you cannot simply wish away.

None of that automatically makes politics dirty. Governing a plural society requires compromise. A person who refuses every compromise may feel pure and accomplish very little.

What troubled me was a different kind of compromise: the kind that asks you to become quiet about something you know is wrong because the person responsible is useful. The kind that turns public service into exchange. Support for access. Loyalty for protection. Favors that later become debts. Relationships that matter more than standards.

I do not claim every politician works that way. That would be lazy, and I do not believe it.

I am saying that the version of politics I had come to see up close enough, through public life and the culture around it, no longer matched the version I had imagined when the dream was simpler.

I became disillusioned.

For a while, that disappointment felt almost like losing a possible future.

When you have imagined a role for yourself long enough, walking away from the role can feel like shrinking the size of your contribution. If I did not enter politics, was I choosing the easier path? Was I becoming one of those people who complains from the outside while refusing the cost of going inside?

That question bothered me because there is truth in it.

It is easy to criticize institutions you do not have to run. It is easy to demand perfect decisions when you do not have to negotiate budgets, laws, competing interests, staff, deadlines, and the consequences of choosing one need over another.

The outside can be self-righteous.

The inside can be compromising.

Neither position automatically owns the moral high ground.

What changed for me was the realization that I had been confusing public office with public usefulness.

They overlap. They are not identical.

A person can serve through education, communication, business, advocacy, technology, culture, research, community organizing, journalism, philanthropy, design, law, medicine, or a hundred other forms of work that shape public life without putting their name on a ballot.

A person can also enter politics with integrity. I am not arguing against that path.

I am explaining why I stopped treating it as mine.

I did not stop loving the Philippines.

I changed the route.

That sentence gave me more peace than I expected.

Because once I separated the title from the purpose, the purpose remained.

I still care whether people understand what government is doing. I still care about how communities are represented. I still care whether local stories disappear. I still care whether public claims are supported by data. I still care about the difference between a program that photographs well and a program that actually works.

None of those concerns require me to be a politician.

They require me to remain a citizen.

That role is less glamorous than elected office because there is no ceremony for it. No title to announce. No official seal. No microphone waiting because your position guarantees attention.

Citizenship is repetitive.

You read. You ask. You verify. You vote. You document. You help. You build. You criticize. You give credit. You change your mind when the evidence changes. You refuse to become a fan of power simply because the person holding it once agreed with you.

Maybe that is enough of a political life for me.

Not apolitical. Not indifferent.

Just unwilling to believe that service begins only after winning an election.

Part of what changed for me was learning to separate my attraction to service from my attraction to the idea of having enough authority to fix things quickly.

Those are not the same desire.

When you see a problem from the outside, power can look like efficiency. If only the right person were in charge, you think, the obvious thing would finally happen.

Public life teaches a harsher lesson.

The “obvious thing” has budgets, laws, constituencies, contracts, histories, competing rights, old promises, new emergencies, bureaucracies, and people whose interests do not disappear because your idea is good.

That complexity deserves respect.

It also deserves scrutiny because complexity can become a convenient hiding place for incompetence and corruption.

I did not lose interest in public issues when I lost interest in pursuing office.

If anything, I became more interested in the machinery.

Where did the number come from?

Who benefits?

Who was consulted?

What law allows this?

What happens after the announcement?

Who maintains the project?

What data was used?

What would count as success?

Those questions feel less romantic than campaigning.

They may be more useful.

## 34. LOVING THE PHILIPPINES WITHOUT LYING ABOUT IT

Countries are easy to love from far away.

Distance edits.

The food becomes memory. The language becomes music. The chaos becomes charm. The frustrations lose detail. Even people who never left can create the same kind of distance by loving an idea of the Philippines more than the Philippines that exists in front of them.

I understand the temptation.

There is comfort in national pride. We want to believe our country is special, our people are uniquely resilient, our culture is naturally warm, our values are somehow purer than everybody else’s.

Some of those descriptions contain truth.

They can also become excuses.

“Filipino resilience” sounds beautiful until resilience is being used to normalize conditions people should not have to endure. “Utang na loob” can describe gratitude and relationship; it can also become pressure. Hospitality can become an expectation that people keep giving even when they have little left. Respect for authority can create order, or it can make questioning power feel rude.

Culture is not a museum display. It contains strengths, contradictions, beauty, harm, history, adaptation, and arguments about what should survive.

Loving the country means being able to hold all of that without needing a simple conclusion.

I do not want a patriotism that becomes defensive whenever a Filipino names a problem.

Criticism is not automatically shame.

Sometimes criticism is what care sounds like when care has run out of patience.

At the same time, cynicism is not intelligence.

There are Filipinos who speak about the country as if everything is hopeless, every institution is corrupt, every public servant is incompetent, every successful person cheated, every local effort is doomed. Cynicism can feel sophisticated because disappointment has evidence.

But total contempt is another way of giving up responsibility.

If nothing can be trusted, then nothing is worth building.

I reject that too.

The Philippines is not only its failures. It is also the people doing ordinary work without national attention. It is teachers preparing lessons, local historians keeping records, families building businesses, health workers, artists, farmers, engineers, organizers, civil servants who take their jobs seriously, entrepreneurs solving small problems, young people learning skills their parents never had access to, communities preserving language and memory because they understand that losing them would be permanent.

A country is always more complicated than its headlines.

This is why I try to separate criticism from contempt.

Criticism asks, “Why is this failing? Who is responsible? What would improve it?”

Contempt says, “Of course it failed. This is the Philippines.”

The first can lead to action.

The second often becomes an identity.

There is a kind of national self-hatred that treats anything foreign as automatically better and anything local as automatically second-rate. Then there is the opposite insecurity, where we become offended by comparison because praise has to be exaggerated to feel like pride.

Neither helps us see clearly.

I want accuracy.

If something Filipino is excellent, say so without needing to call it world-class as if the world must validate it first.

If something is poor, say so without treating the failure as proof that Filipinos are incapable.

If a tradition deserves preservation, preserve it because it carries meaning, not because old automatically means good.

If a system needs change, change it without assuming everything inherited is worthless.

That kind of love is more demanding than pride.

It asks us to grow up around our country.

To stop needing it to be perfect before we serve it.

To stop needing it to be terrible before our disappointment feels justified.

The Philippines is home enough to criticize and home enough to defend from lazy caricature.

I can love it without lying about it.

In fact, I think that is the only kind of love strong enough to be useful.

The Philippines also teaches you how to hold pride and frustration in the same hand.

You can be moved by Filipino creativity and embarrassed by the systems that make talented people leave.

You can admire resilience and still wish resilience were not demanded so often.

You can love hospitality and notice how easily hospitality becomes tolerance for behavior that should have consequences.

You can celebrate family loyalty and still recognize the pressure families can place on individual choices.

You can treasure tradition and still ask whether every tradition deserves to survive unchanged.

None of that requires contempt.

Criticism becomes lazy when it treats the country as hopeless.

Patriotism becomes lazy when it treats criticism as betrayal.

I want something more demanding than either.

I want affection with standards.
