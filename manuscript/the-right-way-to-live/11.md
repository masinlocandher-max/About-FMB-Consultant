When I say para sa bayan, I am not imagining a flag in the background of every decision. I am thinking about ordinary things.

The language we allow to disappear because nobody bothered to document it. The local story people dismiss because it happened far from Manila. The young person who believes opportunity belongs somewhere else. The small business trying to be found. The citizen who does not understand a process because the information was written as if ordinary people were an inconvenience. The community that becomes a talking point before anyone has listened to the people who actually live there.

Country is not an abstraction when you look closely enough.

It is made of places, people, memory, systems, arguments, roads, schools, markets, family names, accents, mistakes, ambitions, and the stories we keep deciding are worth preserving.

This is why I have never been satisfied with the idea that patriotism is mainly about feeling proud.

Pride is easy when something wins.

A Filipino becomes internationally recognized and suddenly everyone wants to claim the victory. A destination becomes famous and we speak as if we personally preserved it. A tradition becomes marketable and we remember that it is ours.

The harder work is caring before something becomes impressive.

To care about the local language while only a few people are paying attention. To care about history before it can be turned into tourism. To care whether information is accurate even when a more dramatic version would travel farther. To care about how a community is represented when there is no immediate reward for getting the details right.

That is closer to the kind of love of country I want.

Not decorative patriotism. Stewardship.

Stewardship is less glamorous because it asks what you are willing to maintain.

It is easy to say we love heritage. It is harder to document it carefully, credit the people who carry it, and resist inventing details just because the invented version is more attractive.

It is easy to say we support local enterprise. It is harder to build systems where small businesses can actually be discovered, trusted, and paid.

It is easy to say we care about young people. It is harder to make information, training, and opportunity less confusing to reach.

It is easy to say the Philippines has potential.

Potential has become one of those words we use when we want to praise a place without committing to what it would take to improve it.

A country does not become stronger because people keep announcing that it could be strong.

Someone has to do the unphotogenic work.

Someone has to check the data. Someone has to preserve the records. Someone has to explain the process. Someone has to build the boring infrastructure behind the beautiful campaign. Someone has to ask why a service is difficult to access. Someone has to notice that the people being represented are missing from the room.

Not everyone has to do all of this.

That is another mistake we make with service. We speak as if caring about the country requires a grand sacrifice. It can, in certain moments, but most of the time country is served through competence applied close to home.

A teacher who teaches well is doing something para sa bayan.

A business owner who pays fairly and treats customers honestly is doing something para sa bayan.

A designer who refuses to invent a community’s identity because a stereotype is easier is doing something para sa bayan.

A person who archives old photographs before they disappear is doing something para sa bayan.

A voter who checks before sharing is doing something para sa bayan.

A citizen who can criticize a leader they support and acknowledge a good decision from a leader they dislike is doing something para sa bayan.

We do not need to agree on every political question to take the country seriously.

In fact, a country becomes fragile when love of country is confused with loyalty to a political personality. Governments change. Parties change. Public figures rise and disappear. The country remains larger than any of them.

That distinction matters to me because I have learned how easily public life can become fandom. People defend a politician with the emotional intensity they would use to defend family. Criticism becomes insult. Evidence becomes secondary to belonging. Admitting that “our side” got something wrong feels like betrayal.

I do not want my love of the Philippines to require that kind of obedience.

I want it to make me more difficult to recruit into blind loyalty.

If something works, I want to be able to say it works even when I dislike the person responsible. If something fails, I want to be able to say it fails even when I once supported the person who promised it. If I do not know enough, I want the discipline to say I do not know enough.

The country deserves more than our pride.

It deserves our judgment.

Para sa bayan, to me, begins there.

My love of country also shows up in small obsessions.

I care about whether local stories are preserved before the people who remember them are gone. I care about whether a language survives not only in academic records but in ordinary use. I care about whether a town is represented by real places and real people instead of generic images that could belong anywhere. I care about how Filipinos find information, how communities tell their own stories, and whether the systems built for people are actually understandable to the people expected to use them.

Those things may not look like patriotism in the traditional sense.

There is no flag involved in fixing a confusing form.

There is no anthem playing while someone verifies a historical detail instead of repeating what sounds good.

There is no parade for making a local business easier to discover or helping preserve words that younger generations may stop using.

I still consider those acts part of loving a country.

A nation is not only the large institutions we argue about. It is also the accumulation of local memory, ordinary work, language, businesses, schools, families, roads, stories, archives, habits, and people who decide that something worth keeping should not be allowed to disappear through neglect.

This is where my creative work and my civic interests stop looking separate to me.

Visibility can be service.

Documentation can be service.

Making information clear can be service.

Helping a place present itself truthfully can be service.

Not every contribution needs to resemble politics to belong to the public good.

## 31. PARA SA KAPWA

It is possible to love humanity in theory and be unbearable to the person beside you.

That is why I am careful with big declarations about service.

“For the people” sounds noble. “For the community” sounds noble. “For the country” sounds noble. The phrases are easy to say because the people inside them are still abstract.

Kapwa is harder.

Kapwa has a face. A name. A schedule. A bad mood. A different opinion. A need that may arrive at an inconvenient time.

It is easy to care about people as a concept. It is harder to treat the actual person in front of you with dignity when they cannot do anything for you.

That is where I think character becomes visible.

How do you treat the guard, the staff member, the applicant, the stranger asking a question you have answered ten times, the person whose English is not polished, the person who does not understand the technology, the competitor you do not like, the person who cannot repay the favor?

Public generosity can be staged. Everyday respect is harder to manufacture for long.

Para sa kapwa does not mean saying yes to everybody. It does not mean becoming endlessly available. It does not mean confusing pity with respect.

In fact, respect sometimes requires refusing the easy performance of helping.

If somebody is capable, let them be capable. Do not reduce adults to people waiting to be rescued. Do not make a community look helpless because helplessness makes your role appear larger. Do not turn somebody’s difficult season into evidence of your goodness.

Help should leave dignity behind.

That standard changes the way you give.

Sometimes the best help is information because information gives a person choices. Sometimes it is access. Sometimes it is credit. Sometimes it is a connection you can make in five minutes that would take another person five months to find.

Sometimes the best help is restraint.

Do not repeat the private story. Do not post the photo. Do not make the other person’s problem your content. Do not insist on gratitude. Do not keep reminding them that you were the one who helped.

There is a reason anonymous generosity feels clean. It separates the act from the applause.

Of course, not every act has to be secret. Visibility can encourage others, raise funds, create accountability, and give a project legitimacy. The question is whether the person being helped remains a person or becomes a prop.

Kapwa also complicates ambition in a useful way.

A person can be intensely ambitious and still make room for others. I do not see generosity and excellence as opposites. I do not believe we have to choose between building ourselves and caring about who gets left behind.

The better question is what kind of success our ambition creates around us.

Does your growth create opportunity, or only distance?

Do people become stronger from working with you, or more dependent on your approval?

Do you share credit accurately?

Do you pay people fairly when you can afford to?

Do you make systems clearer or intentionally confusing so you remain the gatekeeper?

Do you know when to step forward and when the person whose story is being told should be the one at the center?

These questions are not about being saintly. I do not trust sainthood as a management strategy.

They are about the social consequences of the way we build our lives.

We affect people even when our intention is private.

A boss’s insecurity becomes an employee’s anxiety. A parent’s fear can become a child’s limit. A leader’s vanity can become everybody else’s wasted time. A competent person’s generosity can become another person’s first real opportunity.

We leak into one another’s lives.

Para sa kapwa is the decision to pay attention to that fact.

I do not have to carry everybody. I do not even have to like everybody.

But I want to move through the world without treating other people as obstacles, audiences, or instruments.

Sometimes the most human thing we can offer is simply the refusal to make another person’s life smaller so ours can feel larger.

Kapwa also asks something uncomfortable of ambition.

It asks whether I can recognize another person’s humanity when they are inconvenient to me.

It is easy to be generous toward people who admire you. Easy to support someone whose success also makes you look good. Easy to talk about community when the community agrees.

The test becomes different when a person is slower, difficult, critical, competing for the same opportunity, or carrying a need you cannot solve.

Respect does not require closeness.

I can refuse someone and still refuse to humiliate them.

I can compete and still acknowledge good work.

I can end a relationship without turning the other person’s entire character into a public prosecution.

I can disagree politically without deciding the other person has no right to dignity.

That standard is harder than friendliness.

Friendliness is a mood.

Kapwa is a discipline.

## 32. PARA SA SARILI

There is a version of service that slowly teaches you to disappear.

You become proud of being the person who can handle more. You are the dependable one, the strong one, the one who always finds a way. People begin to expect your capacity, and because the expectation feels like trust, you keep proving them right.

Until you are tired in a way sleep does not fix.

Para sa sarili used to sound, to me, like the least noble of the three.

For country. For others. For myself.

Placed beside bayan and kapwa, sarili can sound selfish. Smaller. Almost embarrassing.

I no longer see it that way.

If I am going to be useful for a long time, the person doing the serving has to remain alive inside her own life.

That means rest, yes, but it is bigger than rest.

It means protecting the ability to choose.

Keeping enough of your own money that generosity does not put you in danger. Keeping time that does not belong to an audience. Building a private self that does not disappear when the public version is criticized. Allowing yourself to want things that are not easy to justify as service.

You are allowed to enjoy your life.

That sentence can feel strangely radical for people who were taught that worth comes from usefulness.

You do not have to turn every talent into a contribution. Every hobby into a business. Every free hour into productivity. Every painful experience into advocacy. Every beautiful thing into content.

Some things can belong only to you.

That privacy is not selfishness. It is a form of ownership.

Para sa sarili also means refusing to build an identity entirely out of sacrifice.

Sacrifice creates a powerful moral position. The person who gives the most is difficult to criticize. The person who is always helping can hide from questions about what she actually wants. Service can become a place to avoid yourself.

That possibility is uncomfortable because it complicates generosity.
