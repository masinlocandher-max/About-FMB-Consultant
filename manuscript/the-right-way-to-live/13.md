Power is easy to recognize when it comes with a title.

Mayor. Governor. Senator. Secretary. Director. President.

The title tells us where authority lives. It also makes us underestimate the forms of power that do not come with an office.

A person who controls information has power. A person who knows how to make something visible has power. A teacher has power over what a student believes is possible. A business owner has power over the conditions people work under. A journalist has power over what becomes legible to the public. A designer has power over how a place or person is framed. A person with a large audience has power even if nobody elected them.

We should take those forms of power seriously too.

They are not equal to government authority. They do not carry the same legal responsibility. But they still shape lives.

Once I stopped treating politics as the only serious route to service, I began noticing how many public acts happen outside government every day.

Someone creates a clearer way for people to access information.

Someone documents a language before more words disappear.

Someone builds a business that gives people work.

Someone teaches a skill that changes a person’s earning power.

Someone funds a student’s fees quietly.

Someone creates a platform for a local community to be discovered on its own terms.

Someone corrects misinformation before it spreads.

Someone makes public data understandable.

Someone preserves an old photograph, records an elder’s story, or writes down a place name correctly instead of allowing convenience to erase it.

None of these people needs an election certificate to matter.

That realization relieved me, but it also removed an excuse.

If service does not require office, then I cannot wait for office before becoming useful.

I cannot say, “If I had power, I would…” forever.

What can I do with the power I already have?

That question is much less romantic.

It also produces more work.

You may not be able to change national policy. You may be able to make one local process easier to understand.

You may not be able to solve unemployment. You may be able to hire one person fairly.

You may not be able to preserve an entire language. You may be able to document a hundred words accurately and make them easier for the next generation to encounter.

You may not be able to transform politics. You can refuse to spread information you have not checked.

Scale matters, but scale is not the only measure of seriousness.

There is a danger here too. Small acts can become an excuse for avoiding structural problems. Charity cannot replace good policy. Personal kindness cannot fix a broken institution. A scholarship does not solve an education system. A food pack does not answer why a family remains hungry.

We need both levels of thought.

Help the person in front of you, and still ask why the problem keeps reproducing itself.

Build the small solution, and still learn what system makes the solution necessary.

Serve without a title, and still hold people with titles accountable for responsibilities only they can legally perform.

This is where citizenship becomes more interesting to me than political fandom.

Citizenship asks what belongs to government, what belongs to communities, what belongs to institutions, and what belongs to us.

Not everything is government’s job.

Not everything is ours either.

A mature public life knows the difference and refuses the convenience of pretending one side can replace the other.

I may never become a politician.

That sentence no longer sounds like an abandoned dream to me.

It sounds like a clarified one.

I wanted to serve.

I still do.

The work simply does not need to wait for an office.

This is why I count work that improves visibility and understanding as part of service.

A business owner who becomes easier to find can earn more.

A young person who sees her language treated as worth preserving may understand heritage differently.

A resident who can finally locate accurate information without asking five people has gained a small amount of power.

A community represented by its real history instead of an invented marketing story keeps more control over its own identity.

These are not dramatic achievements.

I like them anyway.

I have spent years thinking about brands, communication, storytelling, photography, digital platforms, training, and public perception. None of those skills make me a public official.

They do give me tools.

Service, for me, means noticing where those tools can remove friction without pretending every problem is mine to solve.

Sometimes the useful thing is a campaign.

Sometimes it is a lesson.

Sometimes it is a website.

Sometimes it is a photograph that preserves something.

Sometimes it is asking a question loudly enough that somebody with actual authority has to answer it.

A title changes the scale of what you can do.

It does not create the first obligation to care.

## 36. WHAT I HOPE REMAINS

I think differently about legacy now than I did when I was younger.

I used to hear the word and imagine scale. A name people knew. Work that could not be ignored. Something large enough to prove that a life had mattered.

There is still a part of me that understands that desire. I care about visibility. I believe good work should be seen, especially when visibility creates opportunity, protects memory, or gives an idea the chance to travel farther than the person who started it. There is nothing inherently shallow about wanting your work to matter beyond the room where it began.

But being remembered and being useful are not the same achievement.

A person can be famous and leave very little behind that improves anybody else’s life. A person can also be almost unknown outside a small community and alter hundreds of futures through decisions nobody ever photographs.

So when I ask what I hope remains, I no longer think first about my name.

I think about clarity.

Did I make something easier to understand?

I think about access.

Did somebody enter a room, learn a skill, find information, or see a possibility that had been harder to reach before?

I think about memory.

Did a story, place, language, photograph, or piece of history survive because somebody cared enough to document it accurately?

I think about courage.

Did the way I lived make another person slightly less afraid to begin again, speak clearly, or choose a life that did not fit the standard order?

Those are quieter measures. I trust them more.

Legacy is also not something we control. This is important because people can become ridiculous trying to curate how they will be remembered. They polish every public edge, remove every contradiction, and eventually become a monument to a person who never actually existed.

I would rather leave behind work that feels true than an image that feels flawless.

A human life is contradictory. I have wanted visibility and privacy. Ambition and peace. Independence and help. I have believed things strongly and later changed my mind. I have wanted to serve and, at the same time, wanted a life that belonged to me.

I do not see those contradictions as defects that need to be edited out.

They are the reason this book exists.

I no longer believe one answer can keep fitting a person who is still changing.

Maybe the more honest goal is not to leave behind a perfect answer.

Leave behind better questions.

Leave behind useful work.

Leave behind people who are stronger because your presence did not require their smallness.

Leave behind records that are accurate enough for someone else to build from.

Leave behind an example of ambition that did not need cruelty, generosity that did not need performance, patriotism that did not need blindness, and self-respect that did not require indifference.

That would be enough for me.

And if my name remains attached to some of it, I will be grateful.

If the work becomes larger than my name, even better.

I used to think legacy was what people said after your name.

Now I think it may be what keeps working after your attention has moved somewhere else.

A person you trained who became better than you expected.

A system that no longer needs you to operate every detail.

A language project that makes one more young person curious.

A business that keeps paying people.

A photograph that becomes part of a family’s memory.

A piece of writing that reaches somebody years after you stopped thinking about it.

A community project that survives the personality who started it.

Those things appeal to me because they reduce the need to remain the center.

Being remembered is pleasant.

Being useful after the applause ends is better.

# MY WAY, FOR NOW

These are the things I would keep, at least for now.

Know yourself before applause, criticism, rejection, success, beauty, age, or fear gets the chance to write your definition for you.

Make problems specific. ‘I am not enough’ is fog. Ask what is actually missing.

Do not insult yourself in the name of accountability. You can admit you were wrong without deciding you are worthless.

Capability is not obligation. Not every burden you can carry belongs to you.

Make yourself useful. Let problems become clearer or lighter because you were there.

Say no before resentment has to say it for you.

Hindi ako sagabal sa ambisyon ninuman. Another person’s excellence does not require my disappearance, and mine does not require theirs.

Do not shrink to make insecurity comfortable. Do not inflate yourself either.

Visibility should serve substance. Not every private thing needs an audience.

Beauty can create a first impression. Let competence decide what happens after it.

Finish your studies when you have the opportunity, then stay willing to become a beginner again.

Protect your attention. Not every provocation deserves a piece of your life.

Verify before amplifying. Confidence is not evidence.

Love your country enough to tell the truth about it.

Para sa bayan. Para sa kapwa. Para sa sarili. Let each one correct the excesses of the others.

Service does not begin with a title. Use the power already in your hands responsibly.

Hard work should eventually create some freedom to enjoy the life you worked for.

And when your participation genuinely matters, do not let convenience be the reason you disappear.

I will not stand idly.

# THE RIGHT WAY TO LIVE

You may have opened this book because the title made you a promise.

The right way to live.

That sounds wonderfully certain, does it not? It sounds as if one person can spend enough pages talking about life and eventually hand you the answer.

I have given you my answers anyway.

I told you to know yourself before other people become too loud. I told you not to confuse somebody else’s timeline with proof that you are late. I told you to want more without needing another person to have less. I told you to build enough capacity that your generosity does not destroy you. I told you that education matters, money matters, appearance matters, and none of them can become the final measure of a human being.

I told you about friendship and the danger of letting people you love make decisions you will have to carry. I told you about my mother, the person who stayed even when I knew I had done wrong. I told you that love can disagree with you and still remain.

I told you what I believe about work, reputation, country, service, ambition, silence, and speaking when silence becomes the easier excuse.

I meant all of it.

But here is the part I owe you before you close this book.

There is no single right way to live.

There is no one answer that can survive every family, every wound, every opportunity, every responsibility, every body, every economic reality, every culture, every season, and every version of you that you have not met yet.

If I pretended otherwise, I would be selling you certainty I do not possess.

What I have given you is my way of understanding my life so far.

My mother is my person. Yours may not be your mother.

Education changed what was possible for me. Your route may look completely different.

I care about ambition. You may discover that the life you want is quieter than mine and no less meaningful.

I can tell you what I regret. I cannot know which risk you need to take.

That is the missing variable in every book that tries to tell you how to live.

You.

A real person. A real history. A real set of circumstances.

Advice can give you information. It cannot carry the consequence for you.

A book can show you patterns, give language to something you could not name, or let you borrow another person’s mistakes before paying for the same lesson yourself.

Then the book closes.

Your life does not.

That is where I lose authority over you.

And I should.

You will still get things wrong. I will too. Knowing yourself does not make you impossible to fool, impossible to hurt, or permanently wise. It gives you somewhere to return when you realize you were wrong.

I care more about correction now than perfection.

Correct the route. Correct the assumption. Correct the habit. Correct the story you keep repeating about what you must become.

No one knows the formula because no one stays exactly the same person long enough for a single formula to keep working.
